California Condor

California Condors, otherwise known by their scientific name, “Gymnogyps californianus,” are critically endangered birds found throughout portions of the western United States and northern Mexico. Today, they have a total population of more than 500 birds in captivity and the wild.

Description

California Condors are the largest land birds in North America. They stand approximately 3 to 3.5 feet tall, weighing between 17 and 25 pounds, and possessing wingspans of about 9.5 feet. Adult California Condors have primarily black feathers with white areas underneath their wings and mostly bald heads and necks that can display a variety of different shades. They have an average lifespan of approximately 60 years, though some have been known to live between 45 and 80 years. Condors are social and intelligent scavengers that consume carrion, primarily feeding on carcasses of animals such as deer, cattle, sheep and marine mammals. They locate food using their excellent eyesight and can soar as high as 15,000 feet while traveling up to 250 miles in a single day. Condors reproduce slowly, as they reach sexual maturity between approximately 5 and 7 years of age and generally produce only one egg at a time during breeding.

Threats

Lead poisoning is the leading cause of death among adult California Condors in the wild. Since these birds feed on animal carcasses, they often consume pieces of spent lead ammunition that are left inside deceased, hunted animals. They also encounter a lot of human trash and may feed small pieces of glass and metal to their young, resulting in injury, starvation, and death. Human development and severe wildfires are more threats that California Condors face as these factors degrade areas that Condors use for feeding, nesting, and roosting.

Protection

By 1987, all remaining wild California Condors had been placed into a captive breeding program to prevent their extinction. Reintroduction into the wild began in 1992 and continues today. The U.S. Fish and Wildlife Service works alongside public and private partners through the California Condor Recovery Program to breed and restore Condors to the wild. Conservation efforts also focus on reducing lead exposure, with organizations such as Defenders of Wildlife advocating for the use of non-lead ammunition and protection of important Condor habitat.
