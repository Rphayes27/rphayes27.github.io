Kākāpō

Kākāpō, otherwise known by their scientific name, “Strigops habroptilus,” are large, flightless parrots found exclusively in New Zealand. With only 325 individuals alive today, they are critically endangered with all remaining Kakapo being closely protected and observed.

Description

Kākāpō are large, green parrots recognisable by their owl-like faces and waddling walks. They are the heaviest species of parrot in the world, with male Kākāpō reaching weights of up to 9 pounds. They are also considered to be extremely long-lived birds as their average lifespan ranges between 45 and 60 years, though many have been known to live for up to 90 or 100 years. Unlike most parrot species, Kākāpō are flightless and spend most of their lives on the ground. Their wings are used for balance and to break the parrots’ fall when leaping from branches. They are also nocturnal creatures, becoming primarily active at night where they move around their roost and eat native plants, fruit, and pollen. They're also unique in their breeding habits, as they are the only species of parrot in the world known to use a lek breeding system. During their breeding season the male Kākāpō meet in a traditional area to show off and compete for visiting females.

Threats

Kākāpō were once widespread throughout New Zealand, however, after the arrival of humans, their population declined dramatically. These parrots were targeted by hunters for their meat, skin and feathers, and, once they became known as scientific curiosities, thousands were killed or captured to bring displays to museums, collections, and zoos. Additionally, the introduction of humans resulted in habitat destruction as settlers cleared land for settlements, agriculture and grazing. Domesticated animals such as free-roaming cats and other animals such as stoats and rats were introduced and targeted Kākāpō for food.

Protection

Conservation efforts for Kākāpō began as early as in the 1890s, though the species still became nearly extinct during the mid-1900s. Today, Kākāpō Recovery is the leading organization in the conservation efforts for this species. All remaining Kākāpō live on protected, predator-free offshore islands or within fenced mainland sanctuaries. Conservationists closely manage the population and use a variety of different techniques to better improve breeding and hatching success.
