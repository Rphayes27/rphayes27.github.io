Lemur Leaf Frog

Lemur Leaf Frogs, otherwise known as Lemur Tree Frogs or by their scientific name, “Agalychnis lemur,” are small frogs native to Central America. Due to severe declines in their populations, they are currently classified as a critically endangered species.

Description

Lemur Leaf Frogs have thin bodies and long limbs. They sport a bright yellow-green skin tone with dark speckles and spots scattered around their body. Females tend to be between 4 and 5 centimeters in length, while males reach between 3 and 4 centimeters and their estimated lifespan ranges between 5 and 10 years. Lemur Leaf Frogs are typically found in forests throughout Costa Rica, Panama, and portions of Colombia. As their name suggests, they spend most of their lives among tree branches and foliage. They are strictly nocturnal and have large white and black eyes with vertical pupils, similar to that of a cat's, which help them see at night. Their skin changes depending on their activity, appearing bright green during the day and changing to brown or olive green while they rest camouflaged on leaves at night. Lemur Leaf Frogs primarily move by grasping branches and slowly walking, rather than jumping. Additionally, their diet consists of insects and other small invertebrates.

Threats

One of the biggest threats to the Lemur Leaf Frog is deforestation, as these frogs rely heavily on undisturbed forest for habitat and food. They have also experienced immense population loss due to chytridiomycosis which is a disease caused by chytrid fungus. Chytridiomycosis attacks the keratin rich skin of these frogs causing it to thicken, shed, and become discolored. This is especially fatal as amphibians use their skin for water regulation and breathing, though it seems to impact frogs the most. Although some surviving populations appear to possess resistance to the fungus, the species now occupies only a portion of its former range.

Protection

Thankfully, Lemur Leaf Frog has been successfully bred within zoos and other managed populations. The Smithsonian’s National Zoo maintains a breeding program designed to preserve genetic diversity and establish a population that could be returned to the wild in the future if conditions become suitable. Citizen Conservation similarly works with private breeders and zoos to maintain captive populations and preserve their genetic diversity, providing another population outside of their threatened natural habitat.
