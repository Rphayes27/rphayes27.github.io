Axolotl

Axolotls, otherwise known as Mexican Salamanders or by their scientific name, “Ambystoma mexicanum,” are a species of salamander native to lakes and wetlands in southern Mexico City. With an estimated wild population of only 50 to 1,000 mature individuals, they are classified as critically endangered despite being extremely common in captivity.

Description

In the wild, Axolotls are generally dark brown or grey with mottled coloration, though, when bred in captivity they are typically pink, white, or black. They reach about 9 to 12 inches in length when fully grown and only weigh around 50 to 200 grams. Axolotls are a unique species of salamander, as they never undergo metamorphosis and retain their juvenile characteristics throughout adulthood. Because of this, Axolotls are able to remain completely aquatic, retaining their feathery external gills and long tails through the entirety of their lives. They spend their day hiding within sediment and vegetation before emerging at night to hunt, making them nocturnal creatures. They are also carnivores that feed primarily on worms, insect larvae, mollusks, and small fish. Another aspect that makes Axolotls so unique is their ability to regenerate damaged or lost body parts, including eyes, limbs, and even portions of their brains.

Threats

Habitat loss and pollution are the main population threats facing wild Axolotls. Their former lake habitat surrounding Mexico City has been extensively drained and developed, leaving the species restricted to a small amount of available aquatic habitats. Water pollution caused by development, agricultural runoff, tourism, and waste makes water unsafe and harmful to these salamanders, and drought and increasing temperatures further contribute to their poor living conditions. Additionally, introduced fish such as carp and tilapia compete with them for food while also consuming their eggs and young.

Protection

Axolotls receive legal protection from the Mexican government and are included under Appendix II of CITES. Conservation groups work alongside the Mexican government to restore freshwater habitats and encourage surrounding communities to use traditional farming methods to ensure Axolotls have suitable aquatic habitats. Protected canals have been established which provide Axolotl's refuge from both pollution and invasive fish, while simultaneously helping to improve water quality. Captive breeding and experimental releases have also been implemented to investigate whether populations can eventually be restored to the wild.
