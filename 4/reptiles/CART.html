Central American River Turtle

Central American River Turtles, otherwise known as Hicatees or by their scientific name, “Dermatemys mawii,” are the only surviving species within their family. They are found exclusively throughout areas of Mexico, Guatemala and Belize and are currently classified as critically endangered as their wild population continues to decline.

Description

Central American River Turtles are the largest freshwater turtles found in Central America, oftentimes reaching lengths of up to 24 inches. They have elongated heads and large nostrils, while males are distinguished by the yellowish color found on their heads. These turtles primarily inhabit large rivers, lakes, oxbows and flooded forests. They are almost entirely aquatic, spending most of their lives in water. Specialized structures in their throats allow them to absorb oxygen directly from water, similar to gills. These structures allow them to stay submerged for extremely long periods. Central American River Turtles are also herbivores and survive by feeding on plant matter. The species is particularly unique because its lineage separated from other living turtles around 80 million years ago, with its closest relatives now known only through fossils.

Threats

Unsustainable hunting and habitat degradation are the primary threats that Central American River Turtles face. These turtles are often hunted illegally for their meat, causing their populations to disappear from many areas that were formerly in their range and greatly reducing their populations in the ones that remain.

Protection

Central American River Turtles are a protected species and conservation organizations are consistently working to try to restore their populations. The Hicatee Conservation and Research Center is one such organization working to help these turtles thrive by setting up captive breeding programs and creating assurance colonies. Other efforts are also being made such as reintroduction projects, field surveys, habitat protection and community involvement.
